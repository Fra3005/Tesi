Collecting personal data without notifying the data subject is a sensitive issue heavily regulated by data protection laws. In the Italian legal context, which is governed by the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and complementing national legislation, the principle of transparency is paramount. Therefore, collecting personal data without informing the individual is generally prohibited. However, there are limited exceptions. Here's an overview:

**General Prohibition and the Principle of Transparency:**

The GDPR mandates that individuals have the right to be informed about the collection and use of their personal data. This includes:

*   **Identity and contact details of the controller:** Who is collecting the data and how to reach them.
*   **Purposes of the processing:** Why the data is being collected.
*   **Legal basis for processing:** The justification under the GDPR (e.g., consent, contract, legitimate interest).
*   **Categories of personal data:** What type of data is being collected.
*   **Recipients of the data:** Who the data will be shared with.
*   **Data retention period:** How long the data will be stored.
*   **Rights of the data subject:** The individual's rights to access, rectification, erasure, restriction of processing, data portability, and to object.

This information must be provided in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language.

**Exceptions Where Notification May Not Be Required or Can Be Delayed:**

While direct notification is the standard, there are limited and specific exceptions where informing the data subject might be excused or delayed. These exceptions are narrowly construed and must be justified on a case-by-case basis:

1.  **The data subject already has the information:** If the individual is already aware of the processing activities, providing a separate notice would be redundant. The burden of proof lies with the data collector to demonstrate the data subject's awareness.

2.  **The provision of such information proves impossible or would involve a disproportionate effort:** This is a critical exception, but it is interpreted very strictly. It generally applies when collecting data from publicly available sources or through intermediaries.
    *   **Impossible:** This might occur if the contact information for the data subject is simply unavailable after diligent search.
    *   **Disproportionate effort:** This is assessed considering factors like:
        *   The number of data subjects involved.
        *   The age of the data.
        *   The complexity of informing each individual.
        *   The cost of providing the information.

    Even if disproportionate effort is claimed, alternative measures should be considered, such as publishing the information publicly (e.g., on a website) or providing it upon request. A Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) might be necessary to demonstrate compliance and proportionality.

3.  **The processing is prescribed by Union or Member State law to which the controller is subject and which provides appropriate measures to protect the data subject's legitimate interests:** This exception applies when a law specifically mandates the processing without requiring individual notification. The law itself must provide adequate safeguards for the individual's rights and freedoms.

4.  **Where the personal data must remain confidential subject to an obligation of professional secrecy regulated by Union or Member State law, including a statutory obligation of secrecy:** This typically applies to professions like lawyers, doctors, and therapists, where confidentiality is paramount.

5. **Research purposes, statistics purposes**
If the data collection is for research or statistics purposes, the data owner must be informed about the purpose for which the data are collected and processed.

**Important Considerations:**

*   **Legitimate Interest Assessment:** Even if an exception applies, you must still have a valid legal basis for processing the data, such as legitimate interest. A Legitimate Interest Assessment (LIA) should be conducted to balance your interests against the rights and freedoms of the data subjects.
*   **Data Minimization:** Only collect the data that is strictly necessary for the specified purpose.
*   **Security Measures:** Implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to protect the data from unauthorized access, use, or disclosure.
*   **Documentation:** Thoroughly document the reasons for not providing notification and the steps taken to comply with the GDPR.
*   **DPIA:** Conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) if the processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals.

**In Summary:**

While there are exceptions to the notification requirement, they are narrow and should be invoked only in exceptional circumstances. The principle of transparency is central to the GDPR, and any deviation from it requires careful consideration, justification, and documentation. You should always prioritize informing data subjects about the processing of their personal data whenever possible.
